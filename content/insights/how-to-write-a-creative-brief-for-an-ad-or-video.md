Most disappointing campaigns are not failures of creativity. They are failures of instruction. When the person commissioning the work cannot say clearly what it is for, who it is for and how success will be judged, even a skilled team ends up guessing, and guesses get revised, argued over and billed.

A creative brief is the document that prevents this. It is usually one or two pages, written by the business and given to the agency, production company or freelancer before any creative work starts. A good brief makes the work faster, makes the quote more accurate and makes feedback easier because everyone is measuring against the same page.

## What a creative brief is (and is not)

A brief is **not** a list of things you like. It is not a script, a mood board or a request for "something creative". It describes the problem and the constraints, and leaves the creative answer to the people you are hiring.

It also is not the contract. The quote and agreement come after; the brief is what makes them realistic.

## The sections of a good brief

### 1. Background

Two or three sentences on your business and the situation that prompted the project: a new product, a new branch, a slow season, a rebrand. The team does not need your history; they need the context that explains why this project exists now.

### 2. Objective

What must change because of this work? Pick one primary objective. Examples of objectives that can be judged:

- Increase bookings for weekday dinners.
- Introduce a new product line to existing customers.
- Make the brand better known in a new city.

Objectives such as "go viral" or "look professional" cannot be judged. If you have several goals, rank them. A video cannot do everything equally well.

### 3. Audience

Who are you talking to? Be specific enough that the team can picture them: age range, where they live, what they currently believe about you, what they care about, which platforms they spend time on. "Everyone" is not an audience. If you have more than one, say which matters most.

### 4. The single most important message

If the viewer remembers only one thing, what should it be? Write it as one sentence. Briefs that contain five equal messages produce ads that communicate none of them.

### 5. Tone and personality

How should it feel? Warm, premium, playful, serious, local, modern? Give two or three words and, if helpful, examples of work (yours or others') that capture the feel, along with what you like about them. Be clear about what you do **not** want.

### 6. Deliverables and channels

List exactly what you expect to receive: how many videos or ads, what lengths, which aspect ratios and where they will run (Facebook and Instagram feeds, Reels, TikTok, YouTube, television, your website, in-store screens). Channel decisions shape everything from framing to length, and changing them later is expensive.

### 7. Mandatories

Things that must appear or be avoided: logo, legal text, prices, contact details, a call to action, brand colours, a product that must be shown, words you cannot use, competitors you cannot mention.

### 8. Budget

Share a realistic range. Agencies do not use it to "spend it all"; they use it to propose something that fits. A brief with no budget invites proposals that are either unaffordable or too small to do the job, and wastes time on both sides.

### 9. Timeline

Key dates: when you need to see concepts, when you need the final files, and any fixed external deadline such as a launch or a festival. Include the time your own team needs to review and approve; delays on the client side are among the most common reasons projects run late.

### 10. Success measures

How will you judge whether it worked? Choose a few measures connected to the objective: enquiries, bookings, store visits, leads, cost per lead, video views that lead to a next step. Agree on how and when they will be measured. Being honest about what you can actually measure now is better than promising numbers nobody will track.

### 11. Approvals

Who gives feedback, and who has the final say? One named decision-maker avoids contradictory feedback. State how many rounds of changes you expect.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the solution instead of the problem.** "We want a 60-second film with drone shots" may be the wrong answer to the real question.
- **Too many objectives.** Every extra goal dilutes the work.
- **Hiding the budget.** It rarely produces a better price.
- **Changing the brief halfway.** If the objective changes, say so and expect the schedule and cost to change too.
- **Briefing only the agency.** Make sure internal stakeholders agree on the brief before it is sent. Conflicts discovered during review are expensive.

## How to use the brief once it is written

Send it with a short conversation, not instead of one. A 20-minute call lets the team ask the questions the page cannot answer. Ask them to play the brief back to you in their own words before they start; misunderstandings show up immediately. When feedback arrives later, measure it against the brief: does this change serve the objective, or only someone's taste?
